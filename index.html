The Ocean's Green Pulse

Phytoplankton make about half of the oxygen on Earth, and they tint the sea green as they grow. Satellites read that tint as chlorophyll-a. When and where does the ocean bloom, and why does each region bloom at a different time of year?

Average chlorophyll-a for each calendar month, 2003–2022. Daily MODIS-Aqua images from NASA GIBS were converted back to mg/m³ using the published color scale, averaged into monthly composites on a 0.5° grid (geometric means, cloud-free pixels only), then averaged across years.

Write-up

The question

When does the ocean bloom, and why does the timing differ from place to place? Ocean-color satellites see phytoplankton through the green pigment chlorophyll-a. A single annual-average map shows where the ocean is productive, but it hides the seasons. The North Atlantic and the Southern Ocean can have similar averages and still bloom half a year apart. We wanted a view in which that timing is the main thing you see.

Data and transformations

  • Source. NASA GIBS layer MODIS_Aqua_L2_Chlorophyll_A: daily global imagery from the MODIS sensor on the Aqua satellite. We requested one 720 × 360 image (0.5° per pixel) per day for 2003–2022 through the GIBS WMS API. That is 7,305 images. We also downloaded 2023–2024 but left them out: Aqua's orbit has been drifting toward later, lower-sun overpasses, and 2024 shows a clear winter artifact.
  • Colors → numbers. GIBS serves pictures, not values. Each pixel is one of 255 colors, and GIBS publishes the chlorophyll range that each color stands for (MODIS_Chlorophyll.xml; log-spaced bins from 0.01 to 20 mg/m³). We matched every pixel to its exact color entry and took the log-midpoint of its bin. Transparent pixels (clouds, sea ice, land, gaps between satellite passes, polar night) count as missing.
  • Monthly composites. For each month of each year we averaged all valid daily pixels in log space. That is a geometric mean, which suits chlorophyll because its values are roughly log-normal (see exploration graph 2).
  • Climatology. For each calendar month we averaged the 20 yearly values. A cell is shown only if at least 3 years and at least 20 daily observations had data that month. We also drop a latitude for a month when the sun is less than 18° above the horizon at Aqua's 13:30 overpass. Below that, readings rise in a fake winter "bloom" in both hemispheres (we found this in the zonal means).
  • Derived measures (computed in the browser): vs. annual average = this month ÷ the geometric mean of all months with data at that spot. Peak month = the month with the highest value. Both need at least 6 months of data, so high-latitude cells that are dark half the year drop out. Region and latitude-band averages weight each cell by cos(latitude), because cells shrink toward the poles.
  • Caveats. Chlorophyll stands in for phytoplankton biomass. It is not a direct count. Near coasts and river mouths, sediment and dissolved organic matter push the readings up. Months with heavy cloud are sampled less often.

Design rationale

  • Marks: a raster map plus two linked charts. Chlorophyll is a continuous field, so we draw one rectangle per 0.5° cell on a canvas instead of choropleth regions. The map shows where. The latitude × month panel shows when for the whole planet at once. The line chart puts a few chosen places side by side so their timing can be compared.
  • Projection. We used equirectangular (plate carrée) because it matches the data grid exactly and keeps latitude on a straight vertical axis. That lets the latitude panel share the map's y-axis, so any row in the panel lines up with the same latitude on the map. It exaggerates the area of high-latitude oceans. We correct for that in every average with cos(latitude) weights. We considered Equal Earth (area-true, but it breaks the alignment) and a rotating globe (only half the planet visible, so you can't compare the hemispheres).
  • Color: chlorophyll. The ramp runs from dark navy to blue, teal, green and pale yellow-green. Lightness rises steadily, so "brighter = more chlorophyll" holds even in grayscale. The hues follow what the water actually looks like: nutrient-poor water is deep blue and bloom water is green. The scale is logarithmic (0.02–10 mg/m³) because values span three orders of magnitude, and a linear scale would paint 90% of the ocean the same color.
  • Color: vs. annual average. A diverging scale with a neutral gray midpoint (= the usual value for that spot). Green-teal means above average (a bloom) and brown means below. The two arms are matched in lightness, from ¼× to 4×. Dividing each spot by its own average removes the huge coast-versus-open-ocean differences, so the timing of the seasons stands out.
  • Color: peak month. Month is cyclical: December sits next to January. So we used a cyclic hue wheel instead of a linear ramp. The color fades to gray where the seasonal swing is small, because a "peak month" there is close to meaningless. This makes it a two-variable encoding: hue shows timing and saturation shows how strong the season is.
  • Highlight colors. Up to 3 pinned regions, in amber, magenta and violet. We checked this trio for color-blind separation, and none of them is a hue the map ramps use. Each region also has a numbered badge and a direct label on its line, so identity never relies on color alone.
  • Interactions. A month slider with play animates the seasons, which no single static map can show. The measure toggle switches between absolute amount, relative timing and peak month. Click or brush on the map pins a region and adds its 12-month curve, in the spirit of NameGrapher's dynamic queries. Hover tooltips give exact values and a sparkline. Hovering a latitude band highlights it on the map. Clicking a month in either chart moves the map to that month. The numbered stories set up each view, so a first-time reader lands on the main patterns quickly.
  • Alternatives we considered. Twelve small-multiple maps: a strong static option, but each map was too small to read regional detail, and you still couldn't compare exact curves. Year-by-year maps with a 22-year slider: the year-to-year signal is much smaller than the seasonal one and noisy at 0.5°, so we kept interannual change in our exploration only. Adding sea-surface temperature as a second variable: interesting, but it would have doubled the scope.
